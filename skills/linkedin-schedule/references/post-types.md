# Post Types Reference

This document defines the five post types used in LinkedIn content schedules. Each type serves a different purpose in the series and works best with specific source material and hook patterns.

The taxonomy comes from analyzing how Peter Walker and Kyle Poyar structure their content over time. Neither creator posts the same type of content twice in a row. They alternate between "here's a big number," "let me go deeper on one thing," "here's something that'll surprise you," and "here's what to do about it." That rhythm is what keeps followers engaged across a multi-post series.

---

## 1. Hero Data Post

**Purpose:** Anchor the series. Cast the widest net. This is the post with the highest potential reach because it leads with the single most scroll-stopping stat in the entire dataset.

**Best source material:** The headline finding with the biggest gap between what people assume and what the data shows. Look for findings that combine a specific number with a surprising direction. "Hires per dollar dropped 48%" works because people assume more funding = more hiring.

**Recommended hook patterns:**
- **Shocking Number** (default): Lead with the jaw-dropping stat, no context. Let it hang. "Series A companies used to add roughly 1 person for every $1M they raised. Now it's 0.5."
- **Contrarian Data Bomb** (if the finding directly contradicts a popular belief): State the belief, then undercut it.

**Ideal length:** Medium-long (150-250 words). The hero post needs enough room for the full formula: hook, data citation, setup, 3-5 insight bullets, opinion, close.

**Chart:** Almost always uses the "signature chart" of the dataset. The one that tells the whole story in a single image. Usually a trend line or dual-axis chart showing the main finding over time.

**Cross-referencing:** This is the anchor. It doesn't reference other posts. Other posts reference it.

**What makes a bad hero post:** Picking a finding that's interesting but not surprising. If the audience's reaction is "yeah, that makes sense," it's not a hero. The hero should make people stop and think "wait, really?"

## 2. Deep Dive

**Purpose:** Unpack one specific finding in detail. Where the hero post covers the big picture, a deep dive zooms in on a single chart or segment and explains what's going on and why it matters.

**Best source material:** Individual headline findings that have enough supporting data to fill a standalone post. The finding needs its own chart, its own "so what," and ideally its own surprising angle. If a finding only works as a bullet point inside the hero post, it's not deep-dive material.

**Recommended hook patterns:**
- **Provocative Question** (default): Frame the finding as a question the audience wonders about. "The 2024 hiring recovery looked real. So why did it reverse in two quarters?"
- **"New Data" Signal**: Works well when the deep dive reveals a segment breakdown or cut of the data that hasn't been shared yet. "New data on how hiring differs by raise size."

**Ideal length:** Medium (120-200 words). Focused and tight. One finding, one chart, one takeaway. Don't try to cram multiple findings into a deep dive.

**Chart:** Each deep dive should have its own chart, different from the hero post's chart. If you're running low on charts, a deep dive can reference a specific segment or data point from a broader chart.

**Cross-referencing:** Can open with a brief callback to the hero post: "I shared data last week showing that hires-per-dollar dropped 48%. Here's what happens when you look at it by raise size." Keep the callback to one sentence. The deep dive should stand alone for people who didn't see the hero.

**What makes a bad deep dive:** Repeating the hero stat with slightly different framing. Each deep dive needs to reveal something genuinely new that wasn't in the hero post.

## 3. Contrarian / Myth Buster

**Purpose:** Challenge a commonly held belief using the data. This post type generates the most comments and debate, which boosts visibility for the entire series. It goes mid-series (slot 3 or 4) because by then the audience has context from earlier posts.

**Best source material:** The "Anomalies & Surprises" section of findings.md. Look for data points that directly contradict what the audience assumes is true. The best contrarian posts have a "wait, that can't be right" quality.

Examples of good contrarian material:
- "AI isn't replacing engineers. Engineering share is actually UP." (contradicts "AI will eliminate coding jobs")
- "The minimum viable team to raise a Series A hasn't changed in 4 years." (contradicts "AI lets you build with fewer people")
- "Companies that hire slower after raising actually grow faster." (contradicts "move fast and scale the team")

**Recommended hook patterns:**
- **Contrarian Data Bomb** (always): This is the defining move. State the popular belief, then hit it with the data. "Everyone says AI is replacing engineers. The data says the opposite."

**Ideal length:** Medium (120-200 words). The contrarian post doesn't need a long setup. The tension between the belief and the data IS the content. State the belief, show the data, give your take on why the belief is wrong (or more nuanced than people think).

**Chart:** Uses the chart that most directly illustrates the contradiction. Often the same chart type as a deep dive but highlighting the specific data point that surprises.

**Cross-referencing:** Can reference the hero post's broader trend and then pivot: "The bigger story is that companies are hiring fewer people per dollar. But here's the part that surprised me..." The contrarian should feel like a "plot twist" in the series.

**What makes a bad contrarian post:** Picking a "contrarian" take that isn't actually surprising. "Not all startups fail" is not contrarian. The belief you're challenging needs to be something a meaningful portion of the audience actually holds.

## 4. Tactical Takeaway

**Purpose:** Convert "interesting data" into "here's what to do about it." This is the most practically useful post in the series and tends to get saved and shared by practitioners who are actually making the decisions the data informs.

**Best source material:** The Implications section of article.md, or actionable conclusions drawn from the findings. The key is specificity. "Think carefully about hiring" is not a tactical takeaway. "Plan for 9-12 net new hires in year one, not 13-15" is.

**Recommended hook patterns:**
- **Direct Address** (always): Call out the target audience by name and give them a directive. "Founders building a hiring plan: the benchmarks you're using are 3 years old." or "VCs helping portfolio companies plan headcount: here's what the data says about post-raise hiring in 2025."

**Ideal length:** Short-medium (100-180 words). Practitioners are busy. Lead with the recommendation, support it with 2-3 data points, close warm. Don't re-explain the full dataset.

**Chart:** Often reuses the hero post's chart (since it's the most recognizable visual from the series) or uses a simple comparison table instead of a chart. A tactical post can also work with no visual if the advice is clear and specific enough.

**Cross-referencing:** Can reference the full series: "I've been sharing data on post-raise hiring all week. Here's the practical version." But the tactical post must stand completely on its own. Many readers will see this post and no others.

**What makes a bad tactical takeaway:** Generic advice. "Be thoughtful about your hiring plan" is not helpful. The whole point of having the data is to give people specific numbers and benchmarks they can actually use.

---

## 5. Thread Closer

**Purpose:** Tie the series together and drive traffic to the full article. Only include this for series of 5+ posts. For shorter series, the tactical takeaway serves as a natural ending.

**Best source material:** A brief summary of the series' key findings (2-3 bullets max) plus a link to the full article or report. The thread closer is not a recap. It's a "here's what this all adds up to" with a pointer to go deeper.

**Recommended hook patterns:**
- **"New Data" Signal**: "Full report: [Topic] — what [N] companies tell us about [trend]"
- **Warm close**: Start with something personal or reflective, then link. "I've spent two weeks digging into this data and there's one thing I keep coming back to..."

**Ideal length:** Short (80-120 words). The thread closer is the shortest post in the series. It's a capstone, not a deep dive. Quick summary, personal reflection, link.

**Chart:** Can reuse the hero chart or skip the visual entirely. A simple text-based summary can work well here.

**Cross-referencing:** This is the only post that should explicitly reference the series as a whole: "Over the past two weeks I shared data on [topic]. The full analysis is now up." Don't list every post. Summarize the arc.

**What makes a bad thread closer:** A wall-of-text recap that tries to re-summarize every finding. If people wanted the full story, that's what the article is for. The thread closer should create curiosity, not satisfy it.

## Sequencing Cheat Sheet

| Slot | Post Type | Why This Order |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| 1 | Hero | Widest net, establishes the dataset, creates the "series anchor" other posts reference |
| 2 | Deep Dive | People who engaged with the hero want more detail on a specific angle |
| 3 | Contrarian / Deep Dive | Mid-series engagement boost from debate, or a second cut of the data |
| 4 | Contrarian / Deep Dive | If you have both, alternate so people don't get two of the same type in a row |
| 5 | Tactical | Late-series conversion from "interesting" to "useful" |
| 6 | Thread Closer | Series wrap + traffic to full report (only for 5+ post series) |

**The golden rule:** Never two deep dives back-to-back. The audience needs variety. If you have 3 deep dives, interleave them with the contrarian and tactical posts.
